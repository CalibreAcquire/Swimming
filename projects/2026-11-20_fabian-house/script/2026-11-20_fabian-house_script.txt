Fabian House is on Cannon Street Road in E1.
It's freehold.
There are two shops on the ground floor, a basement,
and nine flats above:
seven two-bedroom and two three-bedroom.

The seller's family built it.
The site used to be a warehouse they used in the fashion trade.
They knocked it down and put up the block.
As far as I know, he's now retired.

It was sold with the tenants in place.
The rent at the time was 196,800 a year.

——————————

It came from a letter.
Apart from a derelict pub in Shadwell that started this business,
it was the first big sale to come out of our letter campaign.

——————————

It was marketed at 5 million.
The price agreed was 4,775,000,
and it didn't move between offer and completion.

On the rent at the time,
that's a gross yield of about 4.1%.

For a lot of investors, 4.1% in East London sounds low.
So why would anyone pay it?

Because the rent wasn't the full rent.
Let at market levels, the building could produce 278,400 a year.
That's 81,600 more than it was earning.

At the price paid,
the yield on that full rent is about 5.8%.

Think of it like a salary that hasn't had a pay rise in years.
The job is the same,
and the pay catches up when the contracts are renewed.
The buyer is paying for the building as it is,
with that gap built in.

Split across the flats,
the price works out at about 530,000 per flat,
and that's before you count the two shops and the basement.

The buyer was a private landlord
who already owns several blocks in the area.
For him it was one more building on streets he already knows.

He's holding it.
We took over the management,
and the rents have gone up by around 30%.
On what he paid, that moves the yield
from about 4.1% to about 5.4%.

——————————

From agreed offer to completion took around six months.
Then, on the agreed completion date,
the buyer's funds weren't ready.

At that point most sellers have every right to walk away.
This one didn't.
The owner held on for another two months,
and it completed.

There's a lesson for both sides.
If you're buying,
your money has to be ready on the day, not nearly ready.
If you're selling,
the price on the offer is only part of it.
How certain the buyer is matters just as much.

——————————

So what did the buyer get
that nine separate flats wouldn't give him?

First, one freehold title.
No freeholder above him charging service charges or ground rent,
and no lease to extend.

Then there's control.
He decides when to re-let, what to repair and who manages it,
across the whole building.

And then there's stamp duty.
Because the building has shops,
it's bought at the commercial and mixed-use rates.
On 4,775,000, that's 228,250.

If you bought nine flats one at a time as additional properties,
at the same 530,000 each,
the stamp duty would come to around 387,500.
That's about 159,000 more for the same number of homes.

It isn't for everyone.
A building like this means one large cheque, one postcode,
and commercial lending, which usually needs a bigger deposit.
If you want to spread your money across different areas,
separate flats may still suit you better.
